I have prepared a proposal for how we could structure our thirty minutes. My aim is that we leave the stage having said something the audience did not already know.

## Why I propose this structure

Our title is broad. In thirty minutes it invites a tour of energy prices, bureaucracy, skills, trade and AI, each touched for two minutes and none in depth, ending in statements everyone in the room already agrees with. I would like us to avoid that.

So I propose we take one weakness/strength only: Germany's skilled workers. Who pays for them, who replaces them, who learns from them. I will set that up in my opening, and I suggest each of you picks one angle on it, see below (again, this is my proposal and we can decide together).

For the discussion, I have prepared two pairs of theses in opposing directions. I feel that each thesis, taken by itself, is quite agreeable. But within each pair, the two cannot both hold true in their general form. That contradiction is the starting point I propose for the discussion. Nobody can say "that is obvious", because if both halves were obviously right, we would have nothing to discuss.

The organizers allow five minutes per statement. I propose we aim for four and treat five as the hard limit. Every minute we save goes into the discussion, and that is where our panel really creates value.

## Part 1: Opening statements

I suggest three rules for all of us, including me: three slides at most, no company introduction slides, one concrete case from your own company with one number the audience will remember.

To make the four opening statements build on each other rather than overlap, I have prepared one guiding question for each of us. Please treat it as an offer, not a script.

**David Wuttke, TUM.** How does industrial AI affect skills, and what do field and lab studies show? I will present the results from my own research.

**Helmut Ahr, Horváth.** How do you measure the value of skilled workers, or are they first of all a cost line? I would love to see one client case: how did they value skilled workers?

**Sarah Engel, TRUMPF.** Take the Cutting Assistant. What did you want it to do, what happened to the operator's skills, and how did you measure this? I will present evidence that AI assistance makes beginners faster but flattens their learning, so I am especially curious about your observation from the shop floor.

**Peter Bosch, CARIAD.** CARIAD cut a third of its people and built a campus of 1,000 AI experts. Does a software company still need a broad base of skilled engineers, or a few superstars managing a fleet of agents? I am less interested in the target picture than in what the restructuring taught you about who you really need. Will we soon see a transfer market of superstars?

## Part 2: Discussion

I will run the discussion along the two pairs of opposing theses, roughly six minutes each. I present a pair in thirty seconds. Then I ask each of you to take a side, A or B, with one reason or one example, about one minute each. 

The remaining time is for you to disagree with each other. I will keep myself out of it as much as I can.

One request: please avoid "both are right". I built both halves to be plausible. The question I will ask is which one you would bet your company on.

**Pair 1: The boomers. What is industrial AI for?**

A: If industrial AI helps, it is because we can finally afford fewer expensive skilled workers.

B: If industrial AI helps, it is because it catches the boomers' knowledge before it retires.

**Pair 2: Gen Z. What does industrial AI do to them?**

A: For Gen Z, industrial AI is a shortcut to expertise. They start where the boomers ended.

B: For Gen Z, industrial AI is a substitute for expertise. They never have to learn what the machine already knows and will never become as much experts as boomers are.

I designed the two pairs to be independent. You can choose B in the first and B in the second, or any other combination. If the room allows it, I will ask the audience for a quick show of hands before you answer.
